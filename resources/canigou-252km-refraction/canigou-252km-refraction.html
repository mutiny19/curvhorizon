The Canigou over Marseille, 252 km Solving atmospheric refraction from a single long-range photograph

Source panorama: near-infrared, by Antoine Mangiavacca, 12 September 2019, 19:15 CEST, shot from the summit of the Canigou (2,784.7 m) toward the Gulf of Lion. Documented by Marc Bret at Beyond Horizons (photographer's own galleries at klape.fr). The image is not reproduced here out of respect for the photographer's rights; follow the links to view it. This page is an independent analysis of it.

The Genealogy of Knowledge →

k = 0.126
The effective (path-average) refraction coefficient for the 252 km sight line during the exposure, solved from the image itself over four independent targets. 1σ range 0.118–0.134; χ² = 0.18 for 3 degrees of freedom, all four residuals under one pixel. Optical horizon from the summit at this k: 201.5 km. Standard-atmosphere models predict k ≈ 0.14–0.16 for this path, so the evening air refracted a little less than textbook.

measured: pixel heights & plate scale assumed: distances, elevations, R′=R/(1−k) solved: exactly one number, k

One refraction coefficient, fitted once and never adjusted per target, reproduces what is visible and what is hidden across the whole panorama. That is why it belongs beside the laser experiment: it recovers refraction from the image instead of assuming it, on a scene argued over for two centuries, since Baron von Zach recorded the Canigou from Marseille in 1801 (see the Allauch history).

One honest correction first

The panorama was shot through a 1,000 nm filter on a converted camera. That is reflected near-infrared (sunlight bouncing off rock, vegetation, and haze), not thermal emission. Pixel brightness measures NIR reflectance and haze penetration, not temperature, and no degree value can honestly be pinned to a pixel. Any temperature scale placed on this image is decoration, not measurement. (The published false-color renders use a warm "ironbow" palette because it reads well, with legends that say plainly they show relative NIR brightness.)

The method, in one paragraph

Refraction is modelled the standard surveyor's way, by bending the ray a fixed fraction k of the Earth's own curvature, equivalently replacing the Earth's radius R with an effective radius R′. From the observer's height H the optical horizon sits at d = √(2R′H), and a target past it is truncated from below by a hidden zone that grows with the square of the distance beyond the horizon:

R′ = R / (1 − k)  ·  d(horizon) = √(2 R′ H)  ·  h(hidden) = (D − √(2 R′ H))² / (2 R′)

How far a feature stands above the visible sea horizon is what the photograph actually records, and it depends on k. So: measure feature heights above the horizon in pixels, and solve for the single k that reproduces them all. The plate scale (5.92″ per pixel) was fixed from WGS84 geodesic azimuths to four labelled landmarks spanning the 33° frame; the sea-horizon row was found by sub-pixel edge detection at eight positions and each feature referenced to its local horizon, which cancels the gentle stitch bow. Nothing in the pixel measurements assumes a value of k; k is the one thing the fit returns.

Solving k from four targets

Four features with well-known top elevations, spanning 215–268 km and 77–714 m, were measured in pixels above their local horizon. One global k must fit all four at once. It does, to within a pixel each:

A one-parameter χ² fit lands at k = 0.126, χ² = 0.18 over 3 degrees of freedom. Two more features then act as out-of-sample checks pointing opposite ways: Cap Sicié (358 m, 284 km) needs k ≥ 0.19 to clear the curve and is duly absent (bounding k from above), while Super Rouvière's top floors are marginally visible (bounding it from below). The photographer noted capturing Cap Sicié the following dawn "thanks to a big refraction," independently confirming k crossed 0.19 overnight, exactly the nocturnal-inversion regime long-range survey work describes.

The hidden-height signature

Plotted against distance, the truncation is a clean parabola rising from the 201.5 km horizon. A flat surface predicts the flat line at zero: nothing hidden, at any distance. The photograph shows the curve.

Why this is not a fudge factor. k was fitted once, globally, to four simultaneous measurements across 70 km of distance and 33° of azimuth, with three degrees of freedom left over and every residual under a pixel. No target got its own coefficient; no value was revised after a prediction failed. The single revision in the whole analysis corrected an input elevation (Super Rouvière's base), not k. The solved 0.126 sits inside the plain-air band controlled surveys report for elevated, settled-evening paths, far from the −4 to +16 chaos of the ground-grazing layer. The same photograph documents, in the author's dawn frames, the k > 0.19 inversion a few hours later.

What would falsify it

The falsifiable content is the distance law. A flat surface predicts zero hidden height for every target at every distance: the full 224 m of Notre-Dame de la Garde (25 px at this plate scale, not the 6 px observed), complete turbine masts down to a visible shoreline, and Cap Sicié in plain view. What the image shows instead is truncation growing with the square of the distance beyond 201.5 km (12 m missing at 215 km, 180 m at 253 km, 305 m at 268 km, 470 m at 284 km), the specific (D − √(2R′H))²/2R′ signature, reproduced across the whole frame by one number. For refraction alone to mimic that on a flat surface, the air would have to bend light upward with a curvature tuned to imitate a quadratic distance law identically in every direction, and then reverse overnight to reveal Cap Sicié at dawn. No measured atmospheric profile behaves that way.

And the analysis is open to refutation on its own terms: a demonstrably identified feature standing clearly visible below the k = 0.126 concealment line at its distance, or a re-measurement of the four fit features yielding pixel heights outside the stated errors, would break the solution. The pixel coordinates, the formulas, the geodesic geometry, and a recomputable spreadsheet are all provided below so anyone can check the work.

How this connects to the experiment

The Laser Beam Follow Experiment argues that refraction should be measured on the night, not assumed, and that a coefficient chosen after the fact to save a result is a knob, not evidence. This panorama is the same argument run in reverse and in the wild: given a single photograph of a scene people have argued over for two centuries, the refraction is recovered from the image itself, over-determined and falsifiable, and it lands in the physically expected place. The Surface Shape Simulator reproduces the same hidden-height geometry interactively; set its Canigou / Marseille preset and slide k to 0.126 to watch this table appear.
